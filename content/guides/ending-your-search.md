# The end of the search

The last steps are the ones people prepare for least, and they decide what you'll live with
for the next few years.

## When the offer comes

- **Say thank you, and ask for it in writing.** You don't have to answer on the call.
  "Thank you, I'm really pleased. Could you send it over so I can read it properly? I'll come
  back to you by [day]" is normal and expected.
- **Read the whole package,** not only the salary: pension contribution, bonus (and whether it's
  guaranteed), office days, hours, probation, notice period, leave, health cover.
- **Use the year test** (below) before you compare numbers.

## The year test

A higher salary feels great for a few months and then becomes normal. Research on people who
change jobs finds satisfaction rises after a move and drifts back over the following year
([honeymoon-hangover effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeymoon-hangover_effect)). Some
things we don't get used to as easily: a long commute is one ([Stutzer and Frey, *Stress that
doesn't pay*](https://wwz.unibas.ch/fileadmin/user_upload/wwz/00_Professuren/Stutzer_Politische_Oekonomie/Publications/Stutzer_Frey_CommutingStress_SJEa.pdf)),
and a difficult manager or a job with no room to grow tend to be others.

So when you compare offers, ask of each one: **what will still matter in a year?** Hearback's
offer comparison has an "In a year" view that puts commute, hours and flexibility, the manager
and team, and growth alongside pay.

## Negotiating

- Negotiating politely is normal and rarely costs an offer.
- **Ask once, with a reason and a number:** "Based on [ranges in similar ads / the scope we
  discussed], I was hoping for [figure]. Is there flexibility?"
- You don't have to share your current salary. You can say: "I'd rather focus on the value of
  this role. My expectation is [range]."
- If pay can't move, other things sometimes can: start date, a review at six months, extra
  leave, a training budget, an extra home-working day.

## Resigning well

- **Check your notice period** in your contract before you sign anything new.
- **Sign the new contract first,** then resign. Not the other way round.
- **Tell your manager in person or by call,** then confirm in a short, neutral email: your last
  day and an offer to help with the handover. No grievances in writing.
- **Leave well.** People move, and the manager you leave may be a reference, a colleague or a
  referral later.

## If they make a counter-offer

A counter-offer is a compliment, and it can be the right answer. Before you accept, go back to
why you started looking:

- If it was **mainly pay**, a matching offer may genuinely solve it.
- If it was **your manager, your workload, your commute or a lack of growth**, money changes
  none of those, and the reason will still be there when the new salary has started to feel
  normal.

> Would the counter-offer fix the thing that would still bother you in a year?

## Before you start

- Ask for a start date that gives you a real break if you can, even a few days.
- Tell the people who helped you, especially anyone who referred you.
- Update your tracker: mark the offer accepted, and the others as withdrawn. (Withdrawing
  politely keeps doors open.)
